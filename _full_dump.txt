[13] (Normal) ABSTRACT

[14] (Normal) This thesis develops and evaluates a passing network analysis framework for hub player identification and tactical performance evaluation using a dataset of thirty-one real international matches spanning six competitions, three confederations, and both the men's and women's game. The system is implemented as a three-layer framework featuring a Python engine of fourteen analysis modules, a FastAPI backend, and a seven-step React studio offering network construction, centrality computation, tactical zone mapping, and hub analysis. Across all sixty-two team analyses, the hub player ranked within the top three on eigenvector centrality in all but one case, on betweenness centrality in fifty-eight, and on degree centrality in forty-five, while the Grund centralisation index and involvement counts agreed across the dataset. Four ethical tests examined traceability and reproducibility, data handling and privacy, representation and fairness, and misuse and opposition-analysis risk. The findings support the use of deterministic network analysis for identifying spatial and temporal patterns of passing behaviour while highlighting ethical risks in opposition analysis. The project therefore presents the system as a human decision-support tool rather than an autonomous tactical analyst, emphasizing both technical performance and responsible use.

[36] (Heading 1) 3. Introduction

[37] (Normal) Football is the most widely followed team sport in the world, with audiences spanning every continent and culture. The scale of the game's popularity is matched by the scale of its complexity: a single match is the product of hundreds of individual decisions, most of them invisible to the casual viewer. Understanding that complexity has become a discipline in its own right, and one that has been transformed over the past two decades by the availability of detailed event data.

[38] (Normal) Professional clubs now employ teams of analysts to support decisions on recruitment, tactics and training. Event data providers record every action that takes place on the pitch, and metrics such as expected goals (xG) and expected threat (xT) have moved from research journals into mainstream broadcasting. The result is that football analysis has become a data-driven enterprise, in which tactical judgements are increasingly expected to be supported by quantitative evidence rather than by opinion alone.

[39] (Normal) This thesis focuses on one particular analytical lens: the passing network. When a team is in possession, its players are connected by a sequence of passes. If each player is treated as a node and each successful pass as a directed edge, the team's attacking structure becomes a network that can be measured. From this representation, questions that are difficult to answer subjectively can be posed precisely: which player is the team's passing hub, how central is each player to ball circulation, where does build-up play concentrate, and how much threat does it generate? The identification of hub players and the evaluation of tactical performance through network measures form the central concerns of this project.

[40] (Normal) To investigate these questions, a complete analysis framework was designed and developed. The framework is delivered as a standalone studio that builds and analyses a passing network from real match event data from beginning to end. It bundles thirty-one real matches, with expected-threat values precomputed for every action, and fourteen analysis modules implement the underlying computation: network construction, degree, betweenness, eigenvector and clustering centrality, Grund centralisation, weakest-link identification, pressing intensity (PPDA), field tilt, team shape, pass sequences, tactical zone maps, the match timeline and momentum, and an overall match verdict. The studio presents these modules as a guided seven-step workflow in a browser interface and supports the upload of additional matches.

[41] (Normal) The aim of this project is deliberately modest. It does not claim to have discovered a superior tactical doctrine, nor to have built a tool that outperforms professional analysts. Its contribution is a reproducible, transparent framework that turns raw event data into interpretable tactical evidence, together with a systematic account of how that framework was designed, built and evaluated. The emphasis throughout is on the quality of the investigation rather than on any single headline result.

[42] (Normal) The dataset on which the framework is demonstrated is deliberately composed of knockout matches from major international tournaments. Knockout football is high-stakes football: every match can end a campaign, and it is in exactly such contexts that the ability to identify an opponent's hub player and to evaluate a team's tactical performance matters most. The dataset also spans men's and women's international football and three confederations' flagship competitions, UEFA, CONMEBOL and CAF, alongside the two global World Cups, so the framework is demonstrated across a genuine variety of playing styles rather than within a single league's conventions.

[43] (Normal) Two conventions are worth stating at the outset. Throughout this thesis, the term hub player refers to the member of a team with the greatest total passing involvement in a match, defined as successful passes made plus successful passes received. Second, the framework is deterministic: every metric it produces is a direct computation over the event data, with no learned or trained component. This property is deliberate, and it underpins the transparency argument developed in the ethical discussion of this thesis, because every number in the tool can be traced back to the code that produced it and the events that fed it.

[50] (Heading 1) 5. Problem Context and Motivation

[51] (Normal) The motivation for this project stems from a genuine interest in both football and data analysis. Watching the game raises questions that casual observation cannot settle: why a team can dominate possession without creating danger, or why the player who touches the ball most is not always the one credited with shaping the match. Data science offers a way to investigate such questions rigorously rather than impressionistically. This project therefore provides an opportunity to apply the analytical skills developed during the programme of study to a real, data-rich domain. The football context renders the technical work concrete and engaging.

[52] (Normal) The starting point is a practical problem in football analysis: how should a team’s passing behaviour be described, compared and evaluated? Traditional tactical analysis relies heavily on observation and professional judgement. Analysts watch matches, identify patterns by eye, and describe a team’s style in qualitative terms. Such descriptions are valuable, yet they remain difficult to reproduce, difficult to compare systematically across matches, and difficult to connect to measurable outcomes. The problem context of this thesis is therefore the search for a quantitative and reproducible means of characterising how a team passes the ball.

[54] (Normal) The network perspective offers a principled response. When each player is treated as a node and each successful pass as a directed edge, a team’s attacking structure becomes a measurable network. Within this representation the hub player—the individual with the greatest total passing involvement—can be identified unambiguously, and that player’s structural role examined through a family of centrality measures. Degree centrality records how many teammates a player connects to in either direction. Betweenness centrality measures the extent to which ball circulation passes through a given player. Eigenvector centrality scores players according to the influence of those with whom they connect, while the clustering coefficient indicates how densely a player’s immediate neighbours exchange passes among themselves. At the team level, centralisation indices quantify the degree to which circulation concentrates around a single hub, and complementary scores identify players whose removal would most disrupt the network under pressure.

[55] (Normal) Structure alone, however, is insufficient. A team may circulate the ball safely without ever threatening the opponent. The framework therefore augments the network representation with measures of threat and pressure. Expected threat assigns a value to every action according to the change it produces in the probability of eventually scoring, enabling the threat generated by individual players and zones of the pitch to be aggregated and compared. Pressing intensity is assessed by relating the opponent’s successful passes in their own half to the defensive actions performed against them. Field tilt compares the two teams’ shares of final-third activity as an indicator of territorial dominance. Additional modules characterise team shape, pass sequences and directional patterns of play. Spatial mapping locates activity across discrete zones of the pitch, while temporal modules place the analysis in the unfolding context of the match itself.

[56] (Normal) These analytical requirements demand event data of sufficient quality and consistency. The framework is therefore grounded in real match event records that share a uniform schema. Each record describes a single action and supplies the fields required by the analysis: action type and timing, the team and player involved, positional information, start and end locations, pass recipient and outcome where applicable, and associated threat values. Because the underlying structure is consistent, the same set of analytical modules can be applied unchanged to every match. Outputs are rendered as dedicated visualisations—network graphs positioned on the pitch, zonal heatmaps, directional charts and summary cards—so that numerical results are always presented in the spatial and temporal context that makes them interpretable to a coach or analyst rather than to a statistician alone.

[58] (Normal) The practical question that ultimately connects these measures to operational use is one of opposition analysis. Once a hub player has been identified, the framework examines that player’s spatial preferences, involvement in build-up phases, key link-ups and threat contribution, and translates these findings into concrete considerations for mitigation. In this dual capacity—describing a team’s passing structure and supporting the evaluation of that structure—the framework aims to move beyond a simple statistics viewer and to provide evidence that can inform tactical judgement.

[59] (Heading 1) 6. Cognitive Biases in Football Analysis

[60] (Normal) Before detailing the analytical framework itself, it is necessary to address a fundamental problem that affects any quantitative approach to football analysis. Tactical judgement is rarely fully neutral or objective. It does not rest on a complete and unbiased record of everything that occurred on the pitch. Instead, it is shaped by the selective attention, memory and prior beliefs of coaches, analysts and observers. This distinction is highly significant for any system designed to support tactical decision-making, as it fundamentally shapes how every finding in this research must be interpreted.

[62] (Normal) Where information is limited, judgement relies on heuristics, and heuristics introduce systematic biases. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) identified several well-established patterns of judgement under uncertainty, including availability (by which memorable events are judged more probable) and anchoring (by which an initial impression constrains later estimates). In the football context these regularities are readily observable. A spectacular comeback or a high-profile error is more memorable than routine sequences of play and is therefore liable to receive disproportionate weight. An early impression of a player or team can anchor every subsequent assessment. The prospect of being held responsible for an unsuccessful decision may further bias choices towards conservatism.

[63] (Normal) The reputation heuristic constitutes a more specific manifestation of particular relevance to hub-player identification. Because well-known players are more salient in memory, their contribution to collective play is readily overestimated relative to less prominent teammates who may, in structural terms, occupy more central positions within the passing network. When analysis is conducted by eye, reputation can quietly inflate perceived importance. This creates a systematic distortion in which the most visible players appear more central than the event record itself may support.

[64] (Normal) Confirmation bias operates in a complementary manner. As Nickerson (1998) has shown, individuals tend to seek, interpret and recall evidence in ways that confirm pre-existing beliefs. An analyst who anticipates that a particular player will prove central to a team’s structure is therefore likely to accumulate supporting observations while discounting contradictory evidence. Overconfidence frequently amplifies the effect: judgements formed through direct observation are often expressed with greater certainty than the limited and selective sample of events underlying them would justify. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle in which prior expectations shape the interpretation of new information, and the interpretation in turn strengthens the original expectations.

[65] (Normal) Within this thesis, these forms of bias are not viewed as reasons to abandon quantitative analysis. Rather, they serve as critical reasons to remain explicit about the limitations of human judgement and to design analytical tools that mitigate, rather than amplify, those limitations. They justify the decision to build a deterministic system in which every metric is computed uniformly from the complete event record, independent of player reputation or prior expectations. Furthermore, they reinforce the necessity of framing all system outputs strictly as decision-support tools that require human review, rather than as autonomous judgements meant to dictate tactical decisions directly. Every finding presented in the technical results section must be interpreted with these constraints in mind. When the framework identifies a particular player as the hub of a team’s passing network, that output reflects the structure of the event data rather than the salience or reputation of the individual, and the two factors cannot be assumed to coincide.

[67] (Heading 1) 7. Theoretical Frameworks in Football Analysis

[68] (Normal) Just as economic and behavioural theories are utilised to explain why irrational decisions are made under uncertainty in other domains, the same theories can be utilised to explain why tactical judgement in football goes wrong and how a deterministic framework can support it. Furthermore, these theories guide the design choices made throughout this project. Four primary theoretical frameworks are drawn upon in this research: the passing network, information asymmetry, the reputation heuristic, and bounded rationality. The four play two distinct roles. The passing network and bounded rationality directly determine what the framework computes and how it presents it, while information asymmetry and the reputation heuristic motivate the design stance, the open, complete-record, involvement-based presentation that the findings evaluate. Each framework is described below, with specific reference to the role it plays.

[69] (Normal) Figure 5. The four theoretical frameworks drawn upon in this research and how each shapes the design of the framework.

[70] (Heading 3) The Passing Network Framework

[71] (Normal) The passing network framework treats a team as a network in which players are nodes and successful passes are directed edges. The representation is grounded in network science and carries a substantial empirical record: Duch, Waitzman and Amaral (2010) quantified the contribution of individual players from their position in the passing network, Grund (2012) related network structure to team performance, and Gyarmati, Kwak and Rodriguez (2014) used passing patterns to characterise team style. The framework built for this project operationalises this perspective directly. It constructs the directed passing network for every team, computes the four centrality measures, derives the Grund centralisation index and identifies the hub player, so that the theoretical framework is expressed in code and applied uniformly to every match, bundled or uploaded.

[73] (Heading 3) Information Asymmetry

[74] (Normal) Information asymmetry, the economics-of-information concept introduced by Akerlof (1970), describes what happens when one party to a transaction or competition possesses better information than another. In football, the asymmetry is between clubs that can afford proprietary data and analytical staff and those that cannot: better information confers an advantage over opponents who lack it. Public event data and openly documented metrics reduce that asymmetry, because given the same underlying events, any team can reconstruct an opponent's passing structure rather than relying on observation alone. The framework is aligned with this view of data as a levelling instrument, and the alignment is expressed in two design features. First, its input is real match event data in a documented format, and its outputs are reproducible from that data by anyone running the same code. Second, the levelling extends beyond the bundled matches: the framework accepts uploaded matches, in single files or whole workbooks, and applies exactly the same modules to them, so that the information advantage is not gated behind proprietary pipelines or data that only some clubs possess. The provenance of the bundled data is documented in the project files: the two Champions League finals are shipped as individual event files, and the remaining twenty-nine matches are stored in a workbook whose summary sheet records each match's competition, stage, date and scoreline, all in StatsBomb's public event format with expected-threat values precomputed for every action. The project folder contains no redistribution licence for the data, so this thesis makes no claim about its legal status.

[77] (Heading 3) The Reputation Heuristic

[78] (Normal) The reputation heuristic is the behavioural tendency to judge a player's contribution by the salience of their name rather than by the structure of their play. Because well-known players are more memorable, their part in collective play is readily overestimated relative to less prominent teammates who may, in structural terms, occupy more central positions within the passing network. When analysis is conducted by eye, reputation can quietly inflate perceived importance, and the most visible players can appear more central than the event record supports. This heuristic, noted among the cognitive biases discussed earlier, is the specific tendency that bears most directly on hub identification, the core of RQ1, and the framework is designed to make reputation unable to enter the computation. The hub player is defined solely by total passing involvement, a count computed from the event record in which player names serve only as labels, and the four centrality measures are likewise computed from pass data alone; no reputation weight, prior expectation or subjective salience feeds into any of them. The consequence is that when the framework identifies a hub, the output reflects the structure of the data rather than the fame of the individual, and the findings examine whether the involvement-defined hub agrees with the centrality measures and how often the hub is a less prominent player whom an eye-based reading would have overlooked.

[80] (Heading 3) Bounded Rationality and the Decision-Support Argument

[81] (Normal) Bounded rationality is the most general of the four frameworks. Classical economics once modelled the decision-maker as an omniscient optimiser who weighs every alternative and chooses the best one. Simon (1955) argued that this picture is unrealistic: human beings possess limited information, limited computational capacity and limited time, and they therefore satisfice, choosing an option that is good enough rather than searching exhaustively for the best one. Tactical analysis is a textbook setting for bounded rationality. A single match contains hundreds of events and a season contains hundreds of matches, and no analyst can attend to all of them. The practical consequence is that tactical judgements are necessarily formed from a small and selected sample of what actually happened on the pitch.

[82] (Normal) This concept provides the core justification for a data-driven decision-support tool. A coach or analyst preparing for a match cannot realistically attend to every event, and the framework reduces the cognitive burden of the decision by aggregating the complete event record and presenting it in an accessible, spatial form, so that the judgement that remains is informed by the whole record rather than by a selected sample of it.

[85] (Heading 1) 8. Role of Data & ML

[86] (Normal) Data plays the role of evidence base throughout this project. The framework is grounded in thirty-one real matches of event data in which every action is one row, the schema is uniform across all matches, and an expected-threat value is precomputed for every action. Every claim about a team that appears in the findings is computed from this data by the framework itself, so that description and evidence are never separated. The uniformity of the data is what makes comparison meaningful: because the same schema and the same modules are applied to every match, a hub player in a Champions League final can be compared with a hub player in an Africa Cup of Nations semi-final without the comparison being confounded by differences in how the underlying events were recorded or analyzed.

[88] (Normal) A useful distinction, and one that the project observes strictly, is that between being data-driven and being model-based. The framework is data-driven in the sense that every output is a function of the events in the data; it is not model-based in the sense of fitting any learned parameters. The distinction matters because the two approaches carry different guarantees. A deterministic data-driven computation can be audited line by line, whereas a trained model embeds assumptions and data biases inside parameters that are harder to inspect. It is argued in the ethical reflection that, for a tool whose stated purpose is to support human judgement, the auditability of the deterministic approach is a genuine ethical advantage.

[89] (Normal) In the wider field, machine learning has become the most visible face of data-driven football analysis. Learned models are used to estimate expected goals from shot characteristics, to value players, to predict match outcomes, and to characterise playing style. Within the specific literature that this project draws on, network methods have been used to quantify the performance of individual players within a team activity (Duch, Waitzman and Amaral, 2010), to relate network structure to team performance (Grund, 2012), and to search for characteristic team styles (Gyarmati, Kwak and Rodriguez, 2014). These studies show that a quantitative, data-driven perspective can complement expert judgement rather than replace it.

[90] (Normal) Against this background, the choice made in this project is deliberately conservative: the framework contains no machine learning component at all. Its fourteen modules are deterministic computations that take event data and return numbers, with no learned or trained stage anywhere in the pipeline. The choice rests on three considerations. The first is interpretability: because every output is produced by explicit code, every number can be traced back to the events that generated it. The second is reproducibility: the same input always produces the same output, with no training variance to explain away. The third is scale: thirty-one matches is far too small a dataset for reliable learned models, and the questions this project asks are descriptive and analytical rather than predictive. It is telling that the project's dependency list contains no machine-learning libraries at all; the entire analysis runs on general-purpose tools for data handling and numerical computation.

[91] (Normal) The absence of machine learning is a scope decision, not a verdict on its value. The literature review examines what statistical and machine-learning models can offer football analysis, and the conclusion discusses how learned components, such as style clustering or outcome prediction, could extend the framework in future and what would be required to add them responsibly, including larger datasets and proper validation. The decision to keep the current framework deterministic is revisited in the ethical reflection later in this thesis.

[110] (Heading 1) 12. Justification

[111] (Normal) This research is justified on four grounds, each of which is independent of the others.

[112] (Normal) Firstly, the project addresses a real gap in analytical practice. The methods it implements, including network centrality measures, Grund centralisation, pressing intensity and field tilt, exist in the research literature and in professional analytics, but they are typically scattered across research papers, proprietary platforms and ad-hoc scripts. The framework assembles them into a single reproducible pipeline with a documented interface, so that the distance between reading about a metric and computing it on a real match is reduced to starting a local server. This integration is the practical gap that the project fills.

[114] (Normal) Secondly, the project is justified by accessibility. The studio runs entirely in a browser against a local server that is started with two commands, it depends only on open-source libraries, and it ships with real match data so that it works out of the box. Users can add their own matches as single CSV files or entire workbooks, and those matches are then analysed with exactly the same code as the bundled data. In a field in which advanced analytical tools are often commercial and closed, the deliberate openness of the framework makes the methods available to students, coaches and hobby analysts who would otherwise have no access to them.

[115] (Normal) Thirdly, the work is justified by the nature of its data. Real event data is messy: schemas vary between providers, files are incomplete, and not every expected column is present. The framework must handle an upload with nothing more than team, event type and minute, and it must default missing threat values to zero without breaking the analysis. Working against such irregularities develops practical engineering skills that sanitised textbook examples cannot teach, and it means the framework has been tested against the conditions of real matches rather than an idealised dataset.

[116] (Normal) Finally, the project is justified by its ethical posture. Because the framework is deterministic and its outputs are traceable to the code and events that produced them, it stands as an example of data-driven analysis that can be audited from end to end. The traceability is concrete rather than rhetorical: a hub player is not asserted but computed from involvement counts, a pressing figure is not estimated but derived from defined defensive actions, and any number in the interface can in principle be recomputed from the event data by reading the code. In a discipline in which opaque models increasingly inform decisions about players, opponents and tactics, an open and explainable alternative has value in its own right, independent of whether it outperforms those models. This line of justification is developed in the ethical discussion of this thesis.

[117] (Normal) Each of the four grounds connects directly to one or more of the research objectives, and together they cover all four. The analytical gap is identified by Objective 1, the investigation of the theoretical and methodological foundations of passing network analysis, which is what reveals that the methods exist but are scattered, and it is closed by Objective 2, the development of the framework that assembles them into a single pipeline. The accessibility ground is served by the same Objective 2, whose deliverable is the open, browser-based studio that runs with two commands and works out of the box. The realistic-data ground is served by Objective 3, the evaluation of the framework on the dataset, which is what tests it against the conditions of real matches rather than an idealised dataset. The ethical posture is served by Objective 4, critical reflection on the design decisions and their consequences. The justification is therefore not a set of free-floating reasons but an integral part of the research design.

[118] (Normal) Together, these four grounds, the analytical gap, accessibility, realistic data and ethical transparency, justify the investment of a full research cycle in the project. None of them requires the framework to outperform existing commercial tools, which is consistent with the bounded aim stated earlier.

[127] (Heading 1) 15. Research Methodology

[128] (Heading 3) Overall Methodological Approach

[129] (Normal) This desk-based study uses secondary data, iterative software development, and technical and ethical evaluation. All of the evidence the project analyses is already captured in existing match event data, and all of the analysis is performed by software developed for the purpose; no primary data collection, surveys, interviews or experiments with human participants are involved. The methodology therefore has three components: a literature- and theory-based component that supplies the conceptual foundations of the analysis, an engineering component that develops the framework itself, and an evaluation component that assesses the framework against the research questions and hypotheses.

[131] (Normal) The desk-based choice is justified by the nature of the object of study. This project is about passing behaviour as recorded in event data, not about the people who make decisions on or off the pitch. Everything the framework needs, from the location of a pass to the threat it creates, is present in the data itself, and so the most direct way to study the research questions is to analyse that data with reproducible software. A further consequence is ethical: with no human participants and no primary data collection, the ethical profile of the research is greatly simplified. The considerations that remain are nonetheless real and are examined in the ethical discussion.

[132] (Normal) An iterative approach was chosen over a fixed, waterfall-style sequence because analytical methods must be validated against real data before they can be trusted, and the structure of the analysis could not be fixed in advance. The engine's source contains a documented example of exactly this kind of iteration: the definition of the pressing metric was refined after examining the data, because counting every pressure event inflated the metric's denominator by a factor of roughly three relative to counting genuine defensive actions, and the definition was adjusted accordingly, with the reasoning recorded in the code. Iteration of this kind is only possible when the pipeline can be re-run quickly and cheaply, which is one of the reasons the framework was built as a single reproducible pipeline rather than a collection of disconnected scripts.

[133] (Normal) Furthermore, ethical review was embedded as a stage of the development process rather than being isolated at the end of the project as a mere compliance exercise. This structural choice means that analytical decisions were reviewed for their ethical consequences at the point where they were made, while they could still influence the design. After the data-handling conventions were fixed, they were reviewed for the assumptions they introduce about which matches are analysable; after each metric definition was settled, its consequences for the players and teams being described were considered, as the pressing-metric refinement illustrates; and the design decisions with the clearest ethical content, including the diversity of the dataset and the local-only handling of data, were made deliberately and are documented in the ethical discussion. This embedded approach ensures that ethical concerns are proactively identified where they can still influence the framework, rather than discovered after the system has been built in a form that would be difficult to modify.

[134] (Heading 3) Data Collection Strategy

[135] (Normal) The dataset utilised in this project consists of thirty-one real matches of StatsBomb-format event data: the 2018 and 2019 Champions League finals together with twenty-nine further knockout matches from Euro 2024, the 2022 World Cup, Copa America 2024, the 2023 Women's World Cup and AFCON 2023. The matches are bundled as two Excel workbooks, and every action carries a precomputed expected-threat value, so that the threat mathematics runs directly on the bundled data.

[137] (Normal) The decision to use real rather than synthetic match data was driven by two considerations. The practical consideration is that the framework is meant to answer questions about actual football: only real event data carries the genuine structure of passes, pressure and threat that the analysis seeks to measure, and a synthetic substitute could not validate the framework's claims about real matches. The ethical consideration is that the data is existing, published event data used in a documented form; no primary data collection and no fabrication of records is involved, which is a further reason the ethical profile of the research is simplified.

[138] (Normal) Consequently, the dataset was assembled to reflect the structural characteristics of real match play at the highest level. It uses the uniform StatsBomb event schema, and it deliberately spans six competitions across the men's and women's games and three confederations, so that the framework is demonstrated on a genuine variety of playing styles rather than on the conventions of a single league. The data-handling methodology rests on a small number of explicit rules. A match is usable if it provides at least a team name, an event type and a minute for each action; everything else enriches the analysis rather than being required for it. Missing threat values are defaulted to zero so that a partial upload still produces a complete analysis. Player names are mapped to short display names for the interface, while the underlying records are never altered. These rules are applied uniformly to the bundled matches and to every uploaded match, which is what allows the framework to treat all of its inputs alike.

[139] (Heading 3) Development Phases

[140] (Normal) The first phase, data acquisition and preparation, established the evidence base described above: thirty-one matches of StatsBomb-format event data in which every action carries a precomputed expected-threat value. Preparation also included defining the minimum schema that any additional match must satisfy and the conventions for handling missing values, so that the analysis would be robust to data of varying quality.

[141] (Normal) Figure 6. The expected threat (xT) grid as a spatial heatmap: values rise toward goal, so passes into dangerous space add threat.

[142] (Normal) The second phase, engine development, implemented the fourteen analysis modules that define what the framework can compute. Two definitions deserve particular note because they anchor the framework to published methodology. Expected threat (xT) values every action by the change it produces in the probability of eventually scoring, and the engine's build-up analysis uses the 12 by 8 threat grid published by Singh (2018), which assigns a value to every position on the pitch. Figure 6 shows this grid as a spatial heatmap: the highest threat values sit in front of goal, and the gradient across the pitch is exactly what makes a pass that moves the ball into more dangerous space valuable even when it is not a shot. The second definition is the pressing metric PPDA, introduced by Trainor (2014), which measures the passes an opponent is allowed per defensive action; as noted above, its implementation in the engine was refined after observation of the data. The remaining modules, including the centrality measures, Grund centralisation, field tilt and the weakest-link score, are documented in the technical development.

[144] (Normal) The metric families implemented in the engine follow published definitions, and their methodological role is worth making explicit. The network family treats each successful pass as a directed edge between two players and computes, per player, the degree, betweenness, eigenvector and clustering centralities using the standard algorithms, together with the team-level Grund centralisation index; these measures jointly answer the hub identification question of RQ1. The tactical family locates the team's play on the pitch through a 25-zone grid, a per-zone direction analysis and zone-to-zone connection flows, and characterises its style through pressing intensity, field tilt and team shape. The temporal family follows the match as it unfolds, dividing it into windows, tracing momentum and producing an overall verdict from the metrics the winning team led. Together the three families cover the network, the pitch and the match, which is the full scope of tactical performance evaluation.

[145] (Normal) Each metric is computed by explicit code from the event records, and the code is the specification: where a definition required a choice, such as which events count as defensive actions for the pressing metric, the choice and its rationale are recorded in the source itself. This convention, code as specification, is what makes the methodology auditable and is the practical meaning of the traceability discussed earlier.

[146] (Normal) The third and fourth phases built the two remaining layers of the architecture: the FastAPI wrapper that exposes the engine over a single HTTP endpoint with JSON-safe output, and the React studio that presents the analysis as a seven-step workflow. The iterative relationship between these layers and the engine is the reason the architecture separates computation from presentation, so that either can change without disturbing the other.

[147] (Normal) The fifth phase, evaluation, applied the framework to the dataset and produced the findings of this thesis, following the strategy described below. The sixth and final phase, reflection and writing, is where the ethical considerations, the ethical reflection and the corresponding findings were developed. The methodology closes with one further observation that illustrates its iterative character: an enhanced implementation of the hub analysis module was developed as a standalone file alongside the version integrated into the engine, indicating that individual modules were refined and reworked as the analysis matured before the final integrated form was settled on.

[148] (Heading 3) Evaluation Strategy

[149] (Normal) The evaluation follows the criteria fixed by RQ1 and H1. Because the framework is deterministic, the basic reliability property, that the same input always produces the same output, holds by construction, and the evaluation therefore concentrates on the properties that actually require evidence: whether the outputs are consistent across the thirty-one matches, whether the hub player identified by involvement agrees with the supporting centrality measures, and whether the results are interpretable in the spatial context of the pitch. The evaluation is descriptive and analytical rather than inferential: no statistical significance tests are performed, and no claim is made that the framework's view of a team is the correct one, only that it is a reproducible and coherent one.

[151] (Normal) Reproducibility is also a methodological guarantee with practical content. Because the entire pipeline runs locally and is driven by a single command, any output in this thesis can in principle be regenerated from the bundled data by any reader with the same tools, and any uploaded dataset can be analysed in the same way. The evaluation is therefore not a claim about results that only the author could reproduce, but a demonstration that the framework's behaviour is open to inspection by construction.

[152] (Normal) The ethical considerations of this research are bounded by its desk-based design. The project involves no human participants, no primary data collection, and no experimental interventions; the data it analyses are existing match event records. As a result, the considerations that apply are not those of research ethics boards concerned with consent and harm to participants, but those that attach to the analysis of secondary data and to the design of analytical tools. [153] (Normal) The first consideration is data provenance. The framework's input is real match event data in a documented format, and the analysis is fully reproducible from that data by anyone running the same code. The origin of the bundled dataset is documented in the project files: thirty-one real matches in StatsBomb's event format, shipped with the framework, with every action carrying a precomputed expected-threat value and each match's competition, stage, date and scoreline recorded in the dataset summary. One property of the system is relevant to the ethics of data handling regardless of licence: the framework runs entirely on a local server and in the local browser, so uploaded match data is processed on the user's own machine and is not transmitted to or stored by any third party.

[154] (Normal) A further consideration concerns the representation of the sport itself. The dataset deliberately includes women's international football and competitions from three confederations alongside the two World Cups and the Champions League finals. Choosing to build and evaluate a framework on such a diverse set is a statement that the analysis of football is not confined to the men's elite game, and it guards against the common failure of sports analytics to generalise from a narrow, privileged slice of the sport. Whether the framework's methods behave consistently across this diversity is an empirical question, and it is one of the checks performed in the findings.

[155] (Normal) The second consideration is transparency and accountability. Every output of the framework is the result of deterministic computation, and every number can be traced to the code that produced it and the events that fed it. This property, discussed earlier in this thesis, is an ethical one: it means that when a hub player is identified or a weakness is flagged, the basis for the claim can be inspected by anyone, rather than being hidden inside an opaque model. The framework never asserts a result it cannot justify, which is the standard that H2 describes.

[156] (Normal) The third consideration is the potential for misuse. The framework's natural application, opposition analysis, includes the identification of a team's weakest link and the vulnerabilities of its hub player. Such analysis is legitimate and is performed routinely by professional teams, but it can also be used in ways that put pressure on individual players, and the identification of a specific player as a team's weakest link carries reputational weight. The framework itself is descriptive: it reports what the event data shows and makes no normative claim about any player. The thesis does not claim that the framework improves tactical decisions or changes human behaviour; as stated earlier, such an effect is not measured in this project, and no such result is reported. This restraint is itself an ethical commitment, because overclaiming the power of an analytical tool is a form of misleading its users.

[158] (Normal) A related consideration is the communication of uncertainty. The framework's outputs are point estimates: a centrality value, an involvement count, a pressing figure, each reported as a single number without a confidence interval. They are descriptive summaries of one match, not estimates of an underlying truth with measured error, and users who treat them as the latter would be overreading them. The thesis is careful to describe outputs in exactly this way throughout, and the findings report the framework's behaviour without dressing it in statistical language it does not earn.

[160] (Heading 1) 16. Literature Review

[161] (Heading 2) Introduction

[162] (Normal) This literature review situates the project within three interrelated strands of prior work. The first strand concerns the theoretical foundations of football performance analysis: the graph-theoretic literature that treats a team as a passing network, on which the present framework directly builds. The second strand concerns statistical and computational approaches to tactical analysis, tracing a path from early goal-counting models of match outcomes through expected-goals and expected-threat methods to contemporary practice, together with the pressing, territorial and team-shape metrics the framework implements. The third strand draws on the ethical and methodological literature, including behavioural economics and its application to decision-making in sport as well as questions of data provenance, transparency and representation, providing the theoretical background for the design and ethical arguments advanced earlier in this thesis. Selected case studies of passing-network analyses that embody the analytical patterns followed by this project are then examined in turn, and a synthesis draws the strands together and locates the contribution of the present work within the space they define.

[164] (Normal) The review is selective rather than exhaustive. It is guided by two explicit criteria. The first is relevance to the framework: only literature that either supplies a method the framework implements or a theory to which the framework’s design responds is included. The second is verifiability: every source cited is a published work whose bibliographic details have been checked, and no claim is attributed to a source without that verification. Practitioner writings are cited only where a methodology originated outside the academic literature, as with the expected-threat grid of Singh (2018) and the pressing metric of Trainor (2014).

[165] (Normal) The review serves Objective 1—the investigation of the theoretical and methodological foundations of passing-network analysis—and establishes the standard against which the contribution claimed earlier is judged. Each section closes by identifying the gap or opportunity that the project takes up, so that the review functions as a bridge from what is already known to what this thesis attempts.

[166] (Normal) The statistical modelling of football has a history stretching back more than four decades and falls into two broad traditions. The first tradition comprises models that predict match outcomes from estimates of team strength. The second comprises models that measure the process of play itself. The framework developed in this thesis belongs firmly to the second tradition. Within that tradition, one of the most productive lines of inquiry has been the representation of a team’s passing structure as a network. It is with the theoretical foundations of that approach that the review therefore begins.

[168] (Heading 2) Theoretical Foundations of Football Performance Analysis

[169] (Normal) The application of network science to football can be traced to early efforts that treated teams as interaction systems. Grund (2012) provided one of the first systematic demonstrations that the structure of passing networks in the English Premier League is significantly associated with team performance. By representing players as nodes and successful passes as weighted, directed edges, Grund introduced a centralisation index that quantifies the degree to which ball circulation concentrates around a single player. While this work established a clear empirical link between network topology and outcomes, it remained largely descriptive: it identified structural patterns but offered limited guidance on how individual positions within the network differentially contribute to collective success or vulnerability.

[171] (Normal) Subsequent research addressed these limitations by expanding both the conceptual repertoire and the range of sports examined. Buldú et al. (2018) supplied a comprehensive review of passing networks across team sports, consolidating the graph-theoretic framing and highlighting the need for measures that capture different facets of importance. In parallel, Duch et al. (2010) showed that network-based metrics can outperform conventional counting statistics in quantifying individual contributions to team success. Building on these foundations, the literature has operationalised a suite of centrality measures, each reflecting a distinct notion of significance: degree centrality records direct involvement, betweenness centrality (Freeman, 1977) identifies players who act as bridges between otherwise weakly connected teammates, eigenvector centrality (Bonacich, 1987) privileges connectedness to other well-connected players, and the clustering coefficient (Watts & Strogatz, 1998) captures the local density of passing triangles.

[172] (Normal) These advances nevertheless left open the question of how to identify, within a single coherent framework, both the hub through which play most heavily flows and the weakest link whose removal most degrades the network. The latter problem is closely related to the key-player problem studied in network robustness research (Borgatti, 2006). The present framework therefore implements all four centrality measures together with Grund’s centralisation index, enabling simultaneous identification of the hub player and the structural weak point. In this way it moves beyond descriptive association toward a diagnostic capability that earlier network studies only partially achieved.

[173] (Heading 2) Statistical and Computational Approaches to Tactical Analysis

[174] (Normal) The first of these traditions, outcome modelling, treats the match as a black box and estimates team strength from final scorelines. Maher (1982) introduced a Poisson model in which each team's goals follow a distribution governed by attack and defence parameters, and Dixon and Coles (1997) refined this family by modelling the dependence between the two teams' scores and weighting recent matches more heavily. Contemporary machine-learning systems, such as the multi-model approach of Baboota and Kaur (2019), carry the same logic forward with considerably richer feature sets. These models can estimate team strength well, but they abstract away the process of play: a fitted distribution over scorelines says nothing about how a team built its attacks, circulated the ball or pressed its opponent. The framework developed in this thesis belongs firmly to the second tradition, and the remainder of this subsection reviews the process-measuring approaches it draws on.

[176] (Normal) Beyond the structural properties of the passing graph, a second stream of work has sought to quantify tactical behaviour through statistical models of event data. Expected-goals (xG) models marked an important early step by assigning probabilistic value to shots. Yet xG remains inherently shot-centric and therefore silent on the value created (or squandered) by the sequences of passes that precede a shot. Singh (2018) addressed this limitation by introducing expected threat (xT), a spatial successor that assigns a value to every zone of the pitch according to the probability of eventually scoring from that location. By applying a 12×8 grid, xT credits each pass with the change in threat it produces, thereby converting positional event data into a continuous measure of progressive value.

[177] (Normal) Complementing spatial value models are metrics of pressing intensity and territorial control. Trainor (2014) popularised passes per defensive action (PPDA) as a transparent indicator of pressing intensity; subsequent work has paired it with field tilt (the share of final-third passes) to quantify territorial dominance. Team-shape descriptors—width, depth and compactness—derived from players’ mean positions (Frencken et al., 2011; Moura et al., 2012) further characterise spatial organisation. Sequential and directional analyses (pass sequences, progressive passes, directional distributions) have likewise been developed to describe how teams construct attacks.

[179] (Normal) While these metrics collectively provide a rich descriptive vocabulary, many earlier implementations either relied on proprietary data pipelines or introduced machine-learning layers that reduced transparency. A deliberate design decision of the present work is that every metric remains a deterministic, fully documented function of publicly available StatsBomb event data, computed without machine learning. This choice prioritises interpretability and reproducibility over opaque predictive performance, directly responding to the reproducibility and opacity concerns that have accompanied more complex statistical and machine-learning approaches in the literature.

[182] (Heading 2) Ethical and Methodological Literature on Data-Driven Football Analysis

[184] (Normal) The final body of literature concerns the cognitive and ethical rationale for systematic, data-driven analysis. Human performance assessment is subject to well-documented limitations. Bounded rationality constrains the volume of information an analyst can process (Simon, 1955), while availability and anchoring biases cause judgements to be disproportionately shaped by memorable events and initial impressions rather than the complete evidence base (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Reputation heuristics further distort evaluation: well-known players are frequently credited with influence they did not demonstrably exert on a given match.

[187] (Normal) Earlier analytic frameworks often left these biases unaddressed, either by incorporating subjective weightings or by focusing exclusively on high-profile competitions and male players. The present framework responds by design: every metric is computed deterministically from the complete event record, no initial value or reputation weight enters the calculations, and the hub player is defined solely by passing involvement, with names serving only as labels. Questions of data provenance and reproducibility are likewise confronted by restricting the analysis to publicly available event data and by maintaining an open, fully documented methodology executed entirely locally. Finally, considerations of representation are embedded from the outset: the dataset spans women’s football and multiple confederations, and limitations identified during evaluation are reported rather than concealed. In these respects the framework aligns with the broader movement toward accountable and transparent sports analytics, converting recognised cognitive and ethical vulnerabilities into explicit design constraints.

[190] (Heading 1) 17. Case Studies

[191] (Normal) This section examines three published studies, each chosen because it illuminates a different aspect of the present project: the network lens itself, the player-level use of that lens, and the extension of the approach to younger players together with the data-governance questions it raises. The three cases are academic rather than commercial; they are evaluated by the evidence they report, and the facts presented here are limited to what the published texts support.

[193] (Heading 3) Case Study 1: Buldú et al. (2018) — Passing Networks as Dynamical Systems

[194] (Normal) Introduction. Buldú and colleagues argue, in an opinion paper at the intersection of network science and football analysis, that a team can be projected onto a passing network in which players are nodes and passes are links, and that this representation captures aspects of team organisation that individual statistics miss. They distinguish three kinds of passing network, player, pitch-region and player-position, and three scales of analysis: the micro-scale, where degree, eigenvector, closeness, betweenness and clustering measures locate each player's role; the meso-scale of motifs and sub-communities, related to team success and to the identification of leaders; and the macro-scale of the team as a whole, where the centroid, stretch index and average degree describe style and performance. Tracing the lineage from Gould and Gatrell (1977) to Duch, Waitzman and Amaral (2010), they insist that passing networks are directed, weighted, spatially embedded and time-evolving objects.

[195] (Normal) Ethical issues. The case carries the concerns that attach to any quantitative reading of play. A team collapsed into link counts is a reduction: leadership and the unquantifiable qualities of a dressing room are absent from the picture, and a precise-looking metric can be over-read as the whole truth about a player. The analysis also rests on proprietary event data, provided here by Opta, and the paper does not discuss the ownership or consent questions that arise when the recorded actions of individual players are mined at scale. Network scores, finally, are numbers whose construction is invisible to most readers, a mild algorithmic opacity that matters when such numbers travel from research into recruitment.

[196] (Normal) Reasons for success and limitations. The paper's success was to establish passing-network analysis as a serious scientific programme, unifying a scattered literature and, more importantly, identifying what was missing. Its most valuable contribution to this project is its insistence that networks are dynamic: entropy falls as a match progresses, density, heterogeneity and centralisation differ between halves, and time must be treated as a dimension rather than averaged away. It equally stresses that a match is the interaction of two networks, a multilayer problem that remains largely unstudied. The limitations mirror the successes: most studies in the tradition still report a single static network, no agreed partition of the pitch exists, adequate null models are immature, and the two-team interaction is left open.

[197] (Normal) Conclusion. For this project the case is the theoretical warrant for the network lens itself. The centrality measures the framework computes are exactly the micro-scale quantities this paper catalogues, and the temporal and interactive dimensions it identifies are the gaps the timeline, momentum, verdict and hub-analysis modules were built to answer.

[198] (Heading 3) Case Study 2: López-Peña and Sánchez-Navarro (2015) — Who Can Replace Xavi?

[199] (Normal) Introduction. López-Peña and Sánchez-Navarro extend the team-level flow-motif analysis of Gyarmati and colleagues to the individual player. From the five patterns of three consecutive passes they derive fifteen player-level motifs and treat each player's distribution over them as a digital fingerprint of passing style. On data covering five seasons of the English Premier League, three of the Spanish Liga and one of the Champions League, more than three thousand matches and over two million passes, they standardise the fingerprints, cluster the 1,296 players into thirty-seven groups, and define a similarity score between any two players. The results are striking: Xavi sits alone in his own cluster with z-scores above four standard deviations on most motifs, and his closest stylistic peer, Yaya Touré, matches him at only 18.2 percent similarity. The motivating question is practical: which player could replace Xavi?

[200] (Normal) Ethical issues. The case raises the profiling concerns that accompany any quantitative characterisation of a person. A midfielder is reduced to a fifteen-dimensional vector, and the framing of players as replaceable by measured similarity is a mild commodification: the question of who can replace Xavi treats a distinctive performer as an instance of an interchangeable role. The similarity scores are opaque to the players themselves, who cannot inspect or challenge a model that may shape their careers. And the dataset is restricted to the English and Spanish top flights plus the Champions League, so players elsewhere are invisible to the analysis; any scouting use would systematically overlook talent in less covered leagues, a self-reinforcing exclusion the authors acknowledge when they note the leagues they could not include.

[201] (Normal) Reasons for success and limitations. The study succeeds because it demonstrates quantitatively that passing style is a real, measurable individual property: the clustering is coherent, Xavi's isolation is a compelling validation, and the similarity score is a concrete instrument for scouts. Its limitations are equally clear. The analysis is static, averaging over whole matches and seasons. Similarity of style is not replaceability of contribution, the authors note that the team factor, how a player's style would transfer to a new club, remains unmeasured, and the fingerprints depend on how possessions and interruptions are defined, a choice that materially shapes the numbers.

[202] (Normal) Conclusion. The case shows the player-level payoff of the passing-network approach the framework builds on: identifying the players on whom circulation depends is exactly the hub-analysis use case. It also sharpens the project's design stance. López-Peña and Sánchez-Navarro obtain their profiles through learned clustering on millions of passes; the framework instead computes its hub and centrality measures deterministically from a single match, trading breadth for auditability, the trade the ethical discussion defends for decision support.

[203] (Heading 3) Case Study 3: Gonçalves et al. (2017) — Passing Networks in Youth Football

[204] (Normal) Introduction. Gonçalves and colleagues take the passing-network approach out of the senior elite game. Forty-four players from the under-15 and under-17 squads of a Portuguese club played two fifty-minute matches while their movements were tracked by GPS, and the authors combined the resulting passing networks with positioning variables, the regularity of each pair's movement measured by approximate entropy, and the players' Voronoi cells. Their central finding is that the higher-performing teams combined lower betweenness with higher closeness: they depended less on any single player, whereas a team organised around one key player was more easily blocked and more vulnerable. The correlation between passing density and positional regularity was stronger at under-15 than at under-17, suggesting that younger players' movement was more strongly tied to the ball.

[205] (Normal) Ethical issues. This is the only one of the three cases whose ethics are fully documented, and it is instructive precisely because of that. Written consent was obtained from the minors, their parents and the team supervisors; the protocol was approved by a research ethics committee and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki; and the tracking of under-age players' movements was treated as the sensitive data it is. The study's data-availability statement then declines to share the data publicly because of participant consent, a genuine tension between the openness that research norms favour and the promises made to participants, and the same tension the present project faces with its own match data, though with far less sensitive subjects.

[206] (Normal) Reasons for success and limitations. The study's success lies in coupling the network view with movement data and in reporting uncertainty honestly: effects are given with ninety-percent confidence intervals and described by magnitude rather than overstated. Its practical message, identify the players a team over-relies on and design training to diffuse the passing lines, is directly actionable. The limitations are equally explicit: two friendly matches, a small sample, an exploratory design and simulated rather than competitive fixtures.

[207] (Normal) Conclusion. For this project the case does two things. It shows that the network approach works outside the elite senior game, supporting the framework's demonstration across varied competitions and levels. And it models the responsible governance the ethical discussion argues for: consent obtained and documented, uncertainty reported rather than hidden, and data handled with the restraint its sensitivity requires. Its central finding also echoes a result the framework is built to measure: the Grund centralisation index and the weakest-link module quantify exactly the dependence on specific players that Gonçalves and colleagues found to be a weakness.

[208] (Normal) Taken together, the three cases frame the project's position precisely. Buldú et al. (2018) supply the theoretical warrant for the network lens and identify the temporal and interactive dimensions most studies ignore. López-Peña and Sánchez-Navarro (2015) demonstrate the player-level payoff of that lens while illustrating the opacity and scope bias of learned profiling. Gonçalves et al. (2017) extend the approach to younger players and model responsible data governance. The framework is built where the three cases leave off: temporal where the tradition is static, deterministic where the profiling is learned, and open where the underlying data are proprietary or consent-restricted.

[209] (Heading 1) 18. Literature Review: Synthesis

[210] (Normal) The literature reviewed in the preceding sections converges on a clear picture. Outcome modelling, from the Poisson models of Maher (1982) and Dixon and Coles (1997) to the machine-learning systems of Baboota and Kaur (2019), has established that team strength can be estimated, but it abstracts away the process of play. The threat and network traditions, from the value grids of Singh (2018) to the passing-network studies of Duch, Waitzman and Amaral (2010), Grund (2012), Buldú et al. (2018) and the player-level motif analysis of López-Peña and Sánchez-Navarro (2015), measure the process itself, but they remain largely descriptive, academic and fragmented across papers and platforms. The behavioural literature explains why a deterministic, complete-record instrument is a sensible design response to the known limitations of human judgement. And the case studies show that the passing-network tradition, however well established, has not yet produced what this project builds: a free, deterministic, integrated tool that takes a match's events and returns a full tactical evaluation that any user can inspect and reproduce. The published analyses are descriptive and fragmented, static where they should be temporal, learned and opaque where they could be auditable, and built on proprietary or consent-restricted data.

[211] (Normal) The synthesis also reveals three gaps that the framework is designed to fill. The first is temporal: Buldú et al. (2018) argue that passing networks are dynamic objects whose structure changes within a match, yet most network studies report a single static picture; the framework's timeline, momentum and verdict modules are a direct response. The second is integration: the measures a tactical analyst needs are distributed across the literature, and the framework assembles them into one pipeline that runs unchanged on any correctly formatted match. The third is openness: the studies examined above are descriptive research artefacts, learned profiling models, or analyses built on proprietary and consent-restricted data, and the framework is deliberately different, its inputs documented, its computations explicit, and its outputs recomputable by any reader.

[212] (Normal) The review has limitations that should be acknowledged. It is selective, and a broader review could add depth in areas this project touches only lightly, such as tracking-data methods, which are noted as future work. It relies in part on practitioner sources where methodology originated outside the academic literature, as stated in the review's introduction, and those sources carry less formal assurance than peer-reviewed papers. And the synthesis is organised around this project's questions, so it should be read as a working map of the relevant territory rather than as a complete survey. Within those limits, the picture it draws is consistent across the three strands.

[213] (Normal) The synthesis also defines the project's contribution, first claimed earlier in this thesis and now visible from the literature: the framework is not a new method but a new arrangement of verified methods, made deterministic, integrated and open. Whether that arrangement performs as claimed is the subject of the findings.

[214] (Heading 1) 19. Technical Development: Tools & Technologies

[215] (Normal) This section documents what was built and with what tools. The account is deliberately technical and traces each component to the code and configuration files in the project, so that the claims of transparency made throughout the thesis can be checked against the implementation itself.

[216] (Normal) The framework is organised in three layers, each built from general-purpose, open-source tools: a computation layer in Python, an API layer in FastAPI, and an interface layer in React. The choice of general-purpose tools is deliberate, as noted earlier: the entire analytical pipeline runs without any machine-learning library. The tools are described in turn below, followed by the three families of analysis modules they support.

[218] (Heading 2) Python: Core Analytical Engine and Deterministic Computation

[219] (Normal) The engine is a single Python program of over four thousand lines that implements all fourteen analysis modules, from network construction and the four centrality measures to the tactical zone maps and the match verdict. Python was chosen because it is the standard language of data analysis and because the project deliberately requires no machine-learning framework: the entire pipeline is deterministic computation over event data, and the dependency list contains only general-purpose libraries. The engine also serves as its own specification, where a definition required a choice, such as which events count as defensive actions for the pressing metric, the choice and its rationale are recorded in the source code itself.

[220] (Heading 2) Pandas and NumPy: Data Management and Numerical Computation

[221] (Normal) All computation is carried out with pandas and numpy. The event data of each match is held as a dataframe, one row per action, under the uniform schema described earlier; the analysis modules operate on these frames with the vectorised numerical routines of numpy, so that the same code runs unchanged on every match, bundled or uploaded. Because every module is a deterministic function of these dataframes, each output can be traced to the events that produced it, the property that underpins the transparency argument of this thesis.

[222] (Heading 2) Openpyxl: Excel Dataset Processing and Match Loading

[223] (Normal) The bundled dataset is distributed as Excel workbooks, and openpyxl is the library that reads them when the engine starts, registering each sheet as a match. The same library handles uploads: a multi-sheet Excel workbook posted to the upload endpoint registers every valid sheet as its own match, and a single-match CSV is parsed with the same rules.

[224] (Heading 2) Excel Workbooks and CSV: Match Data Storage and Input Formats

[225] (Normal) The framework ships with its evidence base: two Excel workbooks bundling thirty-one real matches of StatsBomb-format event data, with an expected-threat value precomputed for every action and a summary sheet recording each match's competition, stage, date and scoreline. The schema is uniform across all of them, and single-match CSV uploads are accepted so that the same analysis runs on any correctly formatted data.

[226] (Heading 2) FastAPI and Uvicorn: Analytical API and Backend Service

[227] (Normal) The engine is wrapped by a thin FastAPI application served by uvicorn. The server loads all matches once at startup, cleans every response recursively so that NaN and infinite values become JSON-safe nulls, and exposes the analysis through a single POST /api/predict endpoint, together with POST /api/upload for adding matches. This separation means the analytical logic can be tested and extended without touching the interface, and the interface can be redesigned without touching the analysis.

[228] (Heading 2) React and Vite: Interactive Frontend and Analysis Interface

[229] (Normal) The interface is a React single-page application built with Vite. It guides the user through the analysis as a sequence of seven steps, from loading a match through the network, centrality, tactical, hub and match-story views to a printable report, and it renders each step's results with purpose-built visual components: the pitch-positioned network graph, the 25-zone heatmap, the direction sonar and the verdict card, among others. A landing page introduces the tool, and the whole application is served from the same local server that performs the analysis, so that a user's data never leaves their machine, a property the ethical discussion relies on.

[230] (Heading 2) Oxlint: Frontend Code Quality and Static Analysis

[231] (Normal) The frontend source is checked with oxlint, a fast open-source linter, so that the quality gate on the interface code is itself part of the open toolchain rather than a commercial service. Like every other component of the framework, the linting step is open and reproducible.

[232] (Heading 2) Command Dispatcher: Analysis Module Routing and Execution

[233] (Normal) The engine exposes its functionality through a command dispatcher: seventeen commands, including matches, teams, data_profile, network, insights, tactical, tactical_map, zone_directions, zone_connections, timeline, goals, shots, compare, player, hub_tactical, health and upload_csv, are routed to handler functions, and the same dispatcher works both through the FastAPI wrapper and in a standalone stdin/stdout mode. The fourteen analysis modules can be grouped into three families, matching the classification introduced in the methodology.

[234] (Heading 1) 20. Technical Development: Ethical Reflection

[235] (Normal) This section reflects on the development process from an ethical standpoint. The reflection is grounded in the actual decisions recorded in the project, and it treats the development process itself as part of the subject matter of the research, consistent with the second research question.

[237] (Heading 3) Built-in accountability

[238] (Normal) The first and most important property of the process is that accountability was built in rather than added on. Every metric is a deterministic function of the event data, defined explicitly in source code that is part of the project and documented in this thesis, so there is no stage in the pipeline at which a result could have been influenced by an undocumented choice. The engine even records its own decision points: the pressing-metric definition, for example, is accompanied in the code by a note explaining why pressure events were excluded from the defensive-action count and what the data showed, roughly ninety to one hundred and ninety pressure events per team against forty to fifty-five genuine defensive actions across the dataset. A methodology that documents its own revisions in this way is a methodology that can be audited, which is the practical meaning of the transparency claimed earlier.

[239] (Heading 3) Honest reporting of scope

[240] (Normal) The development process was also iterative in a way that deserves ethical comment. An enhanced implementation of the hub analysis was developed separately from the version integrated into the engine, and the two coexist in the project. The thesis documents the integrated version, which is what the served application runs, and it notes the existence of the enhanced work rather than claiming it as part of the shipped framework. This distinction matters because claiming features that are not actually delivered is a form of misrepresentation, and the thesis has been careful throughout to describe only what the project files support.

[241] (Heading 3) Local-first data handling

[242] (Normal) The architecture embeds an ethical choice about data. Because the entire application runs on a local server and in the local browser, no match data, uploaded or bundled, is ever transmitted to a third party, and nothing about a user's analysis is tracked or collected. This local-first design was noted in the ethical discussion; from the perspective of the development process it is a deliberate decision made at the architectural stage, which is the cheapest and most effective point at which to protect users' data.

[243] (Heading 3) Representation and open tooling

[244] (Normal) Two further properties of the process are worth recording. The dataset includes women's international football and competitions from three confederations, and the framework was required to run identically on all of them, a requirement that shaped testing and that the findings examine directly. And the framework is built entirely on open-source tools, so that any claim made in this thesis can in principle be checked by any reader with the same freely available software. Neither property is an accident of the build; both are choices about the kind of analytical practice the project wants to exemplify.

[245] (Heading 3) Reporting limitations

[246] (Normal) Finally, the development process produced its own epistemic lessons, and the thesis has chosen to report them rather than conceal them. The most instructive is a genuine limitation discovered while preparing the findings: the hub player is selected as the player with the greatest passing involvement, and where two players are exactly tied, the choice between them depends on the arbitrary order in which the players were processed, so a tied result is not reproducible across runs. The limitation is small, it is documented in the conclusion, and the findings resolve ties by an explicit alphabetical rule so that every number reported here is reproducible. That a limitation of this kind is found, reported and handled transparently is, in the view of this thesis, what the ethical posture described earlier looks like in practice. The ethical findings assess the second research question against evidence of this kind.

[247] (Heading 1) 21. Findings: RQ1 (Technical)

[248] (Normal) The first research question asked to what extent the framework reliably identifies hub players and characterises tactical performance across a diverse set of matches. The evaluation runs the shipped engine, exactly as delivered, on all thirty-one matches of the dataset, producing sixty-two team analyses, two per match. All numbers in this section were computed by the engine from the bundled event data and are quoted as computed; the raw outputs are reproducible by running the framework as described in the architecture. The findings follow the structure of the investigation: the characteristics of the dataset, the cleaning decisions that prepared it, the temporal and spatial patterns it exhibits, and the network, temporal and hub-level outputs that answer the questions of when and where.

[249] (Heading 3) Dataset Characteristics

[250] (Normal) The dataset comprises thirty-one real matches of StatsBomb-format event data in which every action is one row with a uniform schema and a precomputed expected-threat value. Two of the matches are the 2018 and 2019 Champions League finals; the remaining twenty-nine are knockout matches from five international tournaments, six from the World Cup 2022, five from Euro 2024, seven from the Copa America 2024, six from the Women's World Cup 2023 and five from the Africa Cup of Nations 2023. The set spans six competitions, three confederations and both the men's and women's international game, which is the diversity on which the framework's generality claims rest.

[251] (Normal) The evaluation covers the full dataset rather than a subsample: all thirty-one matches were analysed for both teams, giving sixty-two team analyses, so the diversity described above is exercised in full rather than sampled. The justice score is the verdict metric described in the technical development, 25 points for winning plus 18.75 for each of the metrics the winner led.

[252] (Normal) Two structural properties of the data shape how the findings are reported. First, scale differs across matches: hub involvement counts range from 49 to 227, so raw counts are not directly comparable, and the findings therefore rely on ranks and percentages, the comparable units, rather than raw involvement. Second, the schema is uniform across all thirty-one matches, which is what makes a hub in a Champions League final comparable with a hub in an Africa Cup of Nations final without the comparison being confounded by differences in how the underlying events were recorded.

[253] (Heading 3) Data Cleaning Results

[254] (Normal) The preparation rules are few and explicit. A match is usable if it provides a team name, an event type and a minute for each action; everything else enriches the analysis rather than being required for it. Missing expected-threat values are defaulted to zero so that a partial upload still produces a complete analysis, and player names are mapped to short display names for the interface while the underlying records are never altered. The API layer applies NaN-safe cleaning to every response so that no null value reaches the interface or the analysis modules, and uploaded workbooks are split into their sheets so that every sheet becomes a match analysed with exactly the same code as the bundled data.

[255] (Normal) Two cleaning decisions are recorded here because they are documented corrections rather than silent fixes. The pressing metric was refined during development after the data was examined: counting every pressure event inflated the metric's denominator by a factor of roughly three relative to counting genuine defensive actions, and the definition now excludes pressure events, with the reasoning kept in the code. And where two players tie on the hub-defining involvement count, the choice between them is resolved alphabetically so that every number reported here is reproducible across runs; the six cases in which this rule was needed are reported explicitly below.

[256] (Heading 3) Exploratory Data Analysis: Temporal Patterns

[257] (Normal) The temporal exploration of the thirty-one matches begins with the timeline module, which divides each match into five-minute windows and colours each window by which team was leading on the momentum measure, so the ebb and flow of control across the match is visible at a glance. The engine's tempo measure, passes per minute of estimated possession, adds a second temporal dimension. Across the thirty-one matches, tempo and possession share move inversely: in twenty-six of the thirty-one matches the higher-tempo side was the side with the lower possession share. The extreme cases make the pattern vivid. In the 2019 Champions League final Liverpool played at a tempo of 50.5 against Tottenham's 29.8 while holding only 43.4 per cent possession; in the Euro 2024 final England's 63.5 was the highest tempo recorded in any final, and it came from the side with 43.7 per cent possession against Spain's 72.4.

[258] (Normal) Tempo does not, however, predict the result, and the framework's honesty about that is part of its value. The higher-tempo side won eighteen of the thirty-one matches, barely more than half. The finals illustrate both directions: the higher-tempo side won in 2019 and in the Copa America final, but lost in 2018, where Liverpool's 53.2 exceeded Real Madrid's 42.6, and in the Euro final, where England's tempo advantage of 26.5 passes per minute produced no goal. A reactive reading of any single number would mislead; the temporal family exists precisely to pair such numbers with the context of the whole match.

[259] (Heading 3) Spatial Analysis: Where the Play Happens

[260] (Normal) The spatial counterpart of a hotspot map is, for football, the pitch itself, and the tactical family locates each team's play on a 25-zone grid covering the pitch, five columns by five rows, with a per-zone direction sonar and zone-to-zone connection flows. The most direct spatial summary is the field-tilt measure, the comparative share of final-third successful passes, which sums to 100 across the two teams. In sixteen of the thirty-one matches the winner dominated this measure, decisively in the 2018 Champions League final, where Real Madrid recorded a tilt of 72.3 against Liverpool's 27.7, and in the Africa Cup of Nations final, where Cote d'Ivoire's 75.7 was the largest recorded and Nigeria's 24.3 the smallest. The clearest exceptions are the two against-the-odds finals: in 2019 Tottenham's 58.2 exceeded Liverpool's 41.8, and in the Copa America final Colombia's 61.3 exceeded Argentina's 38.7, so on the framework's spatial measure the losing side owned the final third in exactly the matches where the winner's result flattered its process. Team shape, computed as each team's average width, depth and compactness, completes the geometric reading of where the players stood.

[261] (Heading 3) Network and Centrality Results

[262] (Normal) The network family is the heart of the framework and the direct answer to the hub-identification half of RQ1. In all sixty-two team analyses the engine identified a hub player without error, and the hubs share a striking profile: under the engine's position classification, which assigns players to goalkeepers, defenders, midfielders and forwards by average position, twenty-five of the sixty-two hubs were defenders and thirty-seven were midfielders, and none was a forward. The most involved player in a team's passing network is therefore, across the whole dataset, a player deep in the build-up rather than an attacker. This is precisely the pattern the reputation heuristic would not predict: the hubs are not the famous forwards whose names are most salient, but the less prominent players whose structural centrality an eye-based reading tends to underestimate. Switzerland's Granit Xhaka, with 227 involvements against England in the Euro 2024 quarter-final, was the most involved hub of any team studied, ahead of Croatia's Luka Modric (207); the largest involvement in a final was Real Madrid's Toni Kroos with 159. The dataset also produced six ties on the hub-defining involvement count, including the World Cup final, where France's Dayotchanculle Upamecano and Raphael Varane were level at 106 involvements, a case reported below.

[263] (Normal) The centrality ranks address hypothesis H1 directly, and the evidence is strong but nuanced. The hub was within the top three on eigenvector centrality in sixty-one of the sixty-two analyses and within the top three on betweenness centrality in fifty-eight; it was within the top three on degree centrality in forty-five. The clustering coefficient tells the opposite story: in only four of the sixty-two analyses was the hub among the top five players on clustering, and in only one was it in the top three. The pattern is coherent: the most involved player is a bridge, a connector who sits on the paths that link the team together, which is exactly what high betweenness and eigenvector centrality measure, but it is not a member of a tightly-knit local cluster, which is what the clustering coefficient rewards. In the language of the network literature reviewed earlier, the framework identifies the team's integrator rather than its densest local group.

[264] (Normal) The Grund centralisation index adds the team-level view. Across the sixty-two analyses it ranged from 6.81 per cent, for France in their World Cup quarter-final, one of the ten teams classified as highly decentralised with the ball shared evenly across the side, to 25.94 per cent, for Switzerland in the Euro 2024 quarter-final, one of only two teams classified as highly centralised with a clear playmaker orchestrating play. The interpretation agrees with the involvement data: Switzerland's hub, Granit Xhaka, handled 227 involvements, the most in the dataset, while England, the most decentralised finalist with a Grund index of 10.99 per cent, carried a notably small hub: its most involved player in the Euro 2024 final, Kyle Walker, handled 67 involvements, and the other highly decentralised sides in the dataset carried hubs of a similar order. The index and the involvement count tell the same story, which is the internal consistency H1 predicts.

[265] (Normal) The passing-accuracy and pressing outputs complete the network picture. The winners had the higher passing accuracy in sixteen of the thirty-one matches, most decisively in 2018, where Real Madrid completed 88.6 per cent of their passes against Liverpool's 73.9 per cent, and in the Africa Cup of Nations final, where Cote d'Ivoire's 83.6 per cent followed Nigeria's 69.5 per cent; in the two against-the-odds finals the losers were the more accurate passers, consistent with their dominance of territory and threat. The pressing metric, under the documented definition that excludes pressure events, classified only two of the sixty-two team analyses as high pressing, nine as moderate and the remaining fifty-one as low pressing, with values ranging from 7.8 for Sweden Women's in the third-place match to 48.7 for Australia Women's in the semi-final, and no team as an elite or very high presser. The uniformity of this classification, a direct consequence of the documented definitional choice, is itself a finding: on the framework's measure, these knockout matches were not pressing contests.

[266] (Heading 3) WHEN: Timeline and Momentum

[267] (Normal) The temporal family's headline output is the match verdict, which answers the when question at the level of the whole match: when the process of the match, measured by the metrics the winner led, was actually decided, as distinct from when the scoreline was decided. In ten of the thirty-one matches the winner also led both the field-tilt and expected-threat totals, and the verdict reflected this with a justice score of 100 or 81 under the headline labels deserved victory or clinical domination; the mean justice score across the dataset was 59.8, with six matches at the maximum of 100. The two exceptions in the finals are more informative than the rule. In 2019 Liverpool won despite generating less than half the expected threat of Tottenham (0.465 against 1.067) and a lower field tilt (41.8 against 58.2); the engine returned a justice score of 43 with the verdict label against the odds, meaning the framework recognised that the winner's result flattered its process. The Copa America final produced the same pattern: Argentina won 1-0 while Colombia generated more threat (1.972 against 1.567) and more territory (61.3 tilt), and the justice score fell to 25, the lowest recorded. The verdict metric does not merely ratify the scoreline; it distinguishes comfortable wins from narrow ones, which is the interpretability RQ1 asks about, and the timeline places that judgement in time, showing the five-minute windows in which each side's momentum actually led.

[268] (Heading 3) WHERE: The Hub on the Pitch

[269] (Normal) The where question is answered at the level of the hub player, and the hub analysis module is the framework's deepest spatial output. For each hub it reports the position on the pitch, the build-up phases in which the player is most involved, the preferred spatial zones, the link-ups with teammates and the expected-threat contribution, and it translates these into strengths, weaknesses and concrete mitigation strategies such as man-marking, pressing triggers and the blocking of forward passing lanes. The counts varied sensibly with the underlying data: Switzerland's Xhaka, the most involved hub in the dataset, was credited with five strengths and one weakness; Colombia's Mina, a dominant hub in the Copa America final, with eight strengths and three weaknesses and one of the largest mitigation sets of any hub, five strategies. The most instructive case is Argentina's Enzo Fernandez in the Copa America final, who was the hub despite a negative expected-threat contribution; the engine flagged four weaknesses, among the most of any hub, including the negative threat value, and its mitigation advice was correspondingly pointed. The hub analysis is complemented by the weakest-link module, which scores every player on a position-weighted combination of network integration, press resistance and expected-threat efficiency and identifies the player whose marking would most disrupt the team. Together the two modules answer the where question in the terms an opposition analyst needs: where the team's circulation depends, and which player to press first.

[270] (Normal) One genuine limitation surfaced by the evaluation must be reported rather than smoothed over. Six times across the thirty-one matches, two players tied on the hub-defining involvement count, and the engine's selection between them depends on the processing order of the players, which is not stable across runs. The cases are the World Cup final, where Upamecano and Varane tied at 106 involvements; Sweden Women's in two matches, with Eriksson and Bjorn level at 57 and again at 87; Nigeria in the Africa Cup of Nations semi-final, where Troost-Ekong and Aina tied at 72; France in the Euro 2024 semi-final, where Kounde and Saliba tied at 80; and Canada in the Copa America third-place match, where Kone and Choiniere tied at 75. The thesis resolves such ties alphabetically for reproducibility and reports them explicitly. The limitation does not affect the substance of any other finding, but it is exactly the sort of defect that an evaluation of a real system should surface, and it is carried forward to the limitations.

[271] (Heading 3) Hypothesis H1 Verdict: Supported

[272] (Normal) Assessed against hypothesis H1, the verdict is supported. The hub identified by total passing involvement ranked within the top three on eigenvector centrality in sixty-one of the sixty-two team analyses and within the top three on betweenness centrality in fifty-eight, and within the top three on degree centrality in forty-five. The clustering coefficient is the single contrast: in only four of the sixty-two analyses was the hub among the top five players on clustering, and in only one was it in the top three. This is a qualification of the claim rather than a contradiction of it, because H1 predicts agreement with the supporting centrality measures, and the framework's outputs agree on what kind of player the hub is, the team's connector rather than its cluster core. The Grund index and the involvement counts tell the same story across every match, the outputs were internally consistent across all thirty-one matches including the six women's matches, and the genuine defects, the six order-dependent ties in hub selection, were identified, reported and handled by an explicit rule rather than concealed.

[273] (Normal) Taken together, the findings support RQ1 with qualifications. The framework reliably identifies a hub in every case, the hub is consistently a connector in the network sense, and the tactical and temporal modules produce coherent, interpretable characterisations, including a verdict that can disagree with the scoreline in a defensible way. The qualifications are the coarse position classification, the clustering-coefficient contrast and the tie-breaking defect. The evaluation is descriptive and covers the full thirty-one-match dataset; the conclusion discusses what a wider evaluation would require.

[274] (Heading 1) 22. Findings: RQ2 (Ethical)

[275] (Heading 3) Overview and Methodology

[276] (Normal) The second research question asked what the ethical considerations of using the framework are and how its transparency supports responsible use. The evidence available to this project is of a particular kind: no experiment with human decision-makers was conducted, by design, so the findings here are about the properties of the framework and the practice of the research, not about measured effects on people. Claims that would require user studies or institutional data remain [TO BE PROVIDED]. The assessment is organised as four structured tests, each targeting a distinct dimension of the risk profile, traceability, data handling, representation and the potential for misuse, and the method is empirical in the same sense as the technical findings: the framework's properties are examined directly rather than asserted.

[277] (Heading 3) Test 1: Traceability and Reproducibility

[278] (Normal) The first test asks whether the framework's transparency claim can be verified. It can, and the strongest evidence is the framework's behaviour when its results are awkward. Every number reported in the findings was produced by documented code running on the bundled event data, and the thesis has quoted those numbers exactly as computed, including the six tied hubs and the low justice scores of 25 and 43. Determinism holds by construction: the same input always produces the same output, and where the engine's tie-breaking is order-dependent, the thesis resolves ties by an explicit alphabetical rule and reports them rather than hiding them. The ability to state a limitation precisely, because every output can be traced to the code that produced it, is the ethical property in action: transparency pays for itself exactly when the results are inconvenient.

[279] (Heading 3) Test 2: Data Handling and Privacy

[280] (Normal) The second test examines what happens to the data a user brings to the framework. The server runs on the user's machine, the browser talks to that local server, and no match data, uploaded or bundled, is transmitted to a third party or stored remotely. This is a design property, verifiable from the architecture, and it means that the privacy commitments made in the ethical discussion hold for every user without requiring trust in the operator of a remote service. What the test cannot certify is the provenance of the bundled data itself: the redistribution licence under which the match data may be shared, any formal usage policy for the tool and institutional ethical approval each remain [TO BE PROVIDED]. Those gaps are stated plainly so that the boundary of the finding is exact.

[281] (Heading 3) Test 3: Representation and Fairness

[282] (Normal) The third test concerns who and what the framework represents. The framework ran unchanged on all thirty-one matches across six competitions, including six women's matches, and produced coherent results in every case, with no evidence in the outputs of any methodological failure specific to a competition. A tool that had been designed around the conventions of one league or one level of the game would be expected to degrade elsewhere; the engine's results do not show such degradation, which supports the claim made in the ethical discussion that building on a diverse dataset is a substantive ethical choice rather than a cosmetic one. The women's matches produced no anomalies: their justice scores ranged from 43 to 100, mirroring the men's matches, and their hubs followed the same profile as the men's, deep build-up connectors rather than forwards.

[283] (Heading 3) Test 4: Misuse and Opposition-Analysis Risk

[284] (Normal) The fourth test addresses the risk that the framework's outputs are used to harm. The framework's natural application is opposition analysis: it identifies an opponent's hub, its weakest link and the mitigation strategies that exploit them, which is precisely the information a rival would act on. The risk is managed by the honesty of the outputs. The verdict metric can disagree with the result, as in the two against-the-odds finals, and the framework reports that disagreement rather than smoothing it away; the outputs are point estimates describing single matches, with no confidence intervals and no claim of statistical truth. Unlike a predictive system that feeds its own predictions back into deployment decisions, the framework has no deployment loop: it recommends nothing and acts on nothing, it only describes, and so it cannot amplify its own errors. The remaining responsibility sits with the analyst, and the framework discharges its side of it by never overstating what it knows.

[285] (Heading 3) Hypothesis H2 Verdict: Supported with Bounds

[286] (Normal) Assessed against hypothesis H2, the verdict is supported with bounds. Each of the four tests maps onto a property that H2 names. Test 1 showed that traceability is real: every output can be recomputed from the code and events that produced it, and every limitation, including the six tied hubs, was identified and reported rather than concealed. Test 2 showed that data handling is local by design, so the privacy commitment holds for every user without trust in a third-party operator. Test 3 showed that the framework ran unchanged across all thirty-one matches, six competitions and both levels of the game, with no competition-specific failure, which is the fairness property the diverse dataset was chosen to test. Test 4 showed that the risk of misuse is bounded by the honesty of the outputs: the verdict can disagree with the scoreline, the outputs are point estimates that claim no statistical truth, and the framework has no deployment loop through which its own errors could be amplified. What the findings do not establish, and what no part of this project claims, is that the framework improves the decisions that analysts actually make; that claim requires a user study, and it is [TO BE PROVIDED]. With the provenance, usage-policy and approval gaps stated plainly, RQ2 is answered: the framework's transparency is real and verifiable, and it is the foundation on which responsible use of the tool rests.

[287] (Heading 1) 23. Conclusion & Future Work

[288] (Normal) This thesis set out to design, develop and evaluate a passing network analysis framework for hub player identification and tactical performance evaluation, and to demonstrate it on real match event data. The aim was deliberately bounded, and the work is best judged against that bounded aim: the project delivers a reproducible, transparent instrument, a systematic account of its behaviour, and an honest statement of its limits.

[289] (Normal) What was delivered is a complete framework: an engine of fourteen analysis modules, an API layer, and a seven-step browser studio, built entirely on open-source tools and demonstrated on all thirty-one real matches spanning six competitions. The technical findings show that the framework identifies hub players consistently across every team analysed, that the hub is consistently a network connector rather than a local cluster core, that the tactical modules produce interpretable characterisations including a verdict that can sensibly disagree with the scoreline, and that the hub analysis reaches a level of tactical detail, press resistance, zones, phases and mitigations, that goes well beyond a simple statistics viewer. The ethical findings show that the framework's transparency is a demonstrated property, that its data handling is local by design, and that it runs without bias across men's and women's matches from three confederations and the two global tournaments.

[290] (Normal) The contribution of the work, as defined earlier, is the integration and transparency of the framework rather than any new metric: a free, deterministic, auditable instrument that turns raw events into interpretable evidence, offered as a counterpoint to the opaque and fragmented analysis examined in the case studies.

[291] (Normal) The limitations of the work are stated plainly. The evaluation covers the full thirty-one-match dataset, and it is descriptive rather than inferential: no statistical tests were performed, and the framework's outputs are point estimates describing single matches. The tie-breaking defect in hub selection is a genuine, if small, reproducibility flaw, resolved in this thesis by an explicit alphabetical rule and best fixed in the engine by a defined tie-breaker. The provenance of the bundled data is documented in the project files, though no redistribution licence accompanies it; the usage policy of the tool and any claims about effects on real decision-makers remain [TO BE PROVIDED]. No machine learning is used, by design, which bounds both the framework's power and its demands.

[292] (Normal) Future work follows directly from these limits. The full-dataset evaluation reported here turns the descriptive patterns of the earlier, finals-only analysis into a systematic survey of all thirty-one matches, and the most immediate next step is a wider dataset, including league matches and more competitions, which would test the generality claims more severely. Tracking data would add the off-ball dimension that passing networks cannot see, and learned extensions, such as style clustering or outcome prediction, could be added under the transparency safeguards argued earlier, with larger datasets and proper validation. The tie-breaking defect should be fixed with an explicit rule. And the questions this thesis deliberately left unmeasured, whether the framework changes analysts' decisions and how it is perceived by the people it is about, await a user study that the framework's design makes possible [TO BE PROVIDED].

[293] (Normal) The thesis ends where it began. Football is complex, and the complexity is now recorded in data; the task of turning that data into interpretable evidence is a craft, and the craft is only as good as its honesty. The framework built in this project is a small instrument for that craft, deterministic enough to be checked, open enough to be extended, and modest enough in its claims to be trusted. It does not tell a coach who to pick or a team how to play; it shows, completely and reproducibly, how a team actually passed the ball, and it leaves the judgement to the people whose judgement it is meant to serve.
